## Content and terminology

- Explain difficult ideas with shorter sentences and clearer sequencing, but retain accurate financial and economic terminology.
- Keep terms such as financial investment, capital goods, total return, underlying value, capital preservation, liquidity, inflation, valuation, diversification, opportunity cost, and risk-adjusted return when relevant.
- Keep the focus on personal financial investment in this introductory handout. Do not front-load the IGCSE macroeconomic bridge unless it is needed for a specific task.
- Teach financial investment, speculation, and saving as separate classifications in this course.
- Classify primarily by purpose, evidence, and expected source of payoff.
- Do not classify an action from the asset name alone.
- Do not classify an action as speculation merely because it is risky or short term.
- Do not imply that speculation is necessarily careless or unresearched; careful research can still support a price-movement forecast.
- Do not classify saving as financial investment merely because it earns interest.
- Prefer `gain` or `return` for an individual's trading outcome when `profit` could be confused with the IGCSE Economics meaning of a firm's profit. Retain `profit` for company profits where it is the accurate term.

## Financial-economics accuracy rules

- Use `financial investment` when the personal-finance meaning must be distinguished from the national-income-accounting meaning of `investment`. Phrase the distinction through self-contained definitions rather than student-facing meta-commentary about the handout.
- Define total return as income plus the change in market value over the relevant period. Treat cash flows, productive activity, financial condition and valuation as evidence supporting an expected return, not as components of the investor's return and never as guarantees.
- A capital gain occurs when an asset is sold above its purchase cost or adjusted basis. For Grade 9 wording, use `possible capital gain if sold above purchase price`; do not imply that a price rise is guaranteed or already realised.
- Treat investment and speculation as useful course classifications based mainly on the reason for the trade and the expected payoff source. Acknowledge through wording that the boundary can be imperfect: speculation can use careful research, investment can include capital appreciation, and neither risk nor a short holding period alone decides the category.
- Define saving first as current income not spent now. For the personal-finance comparison, classify money kept mainly for safety and ready access as saving; do not imply that earning interest automatically changes the decision into financial investment.
- Do not call deposits fully protected without a jurisdiction and coverage conditions. Prefer `bank deposit` or carefully qualified deposit-protection wording.
- For bond funds, name credit risk and interest-rate risk rather than writing as though the fund has one issuer that defaults. For an individual bond, distinguish missed interest/principal payments from a market-price fall before sale.
- Fund distributions are not guaranteed and are not identical to investment performance. Use `possible distributions` and keep fees and market-value changes visible.
- Evidence can support an expectation but cannot guarantee a return. Preserve this caveat wherever investment and speculation are contrasted.

## Wording preferences

- Use one main idea per sentence where possible.
- Prefer direct subject-verb structure.
- Define the accurate term first; then explain it in accessible language.
- Write self-contained statements. Never use deictic or document-dependent wording such as `in this handout`, `here`, `above`, or `below` in student-facing explanations.
- Keep definition cards definition-like: use declarative sentences that define the term, identify its essential feature, or state a necessary qualification. Do not insert instructions such as `classify by...` into a definition card.
- Avoid replacing precise terminology with vague everyday substitutes.
- Avoid unnecessarily long qualifications inside a single sentence.
- Use concise bullet examples directly beneath definitions when the examples are primarily illustrative. Use a compact comparison grid when repeated dimensions such as return source and loss mechanism teach an important distinction.
- Normally include at least three examples for each central definition; a fourth is useful when it adds a familiar asset type rather than repeating the same distinction.

## Structure and practice

- Format all four major sections with the same numbered heading style and consistent spacing.
- Prefer one integrated three-way classification grid over separate investment-versus-speculation and investment-versus-saving pages.
- Use repeated criteria across columns so distinctions are visible at a glance.
- Use short scenarios with enough context to identify purpose, evidence, payoff source, risk, and liquidity.
- Include varied practice: classification, justification, misconception correction, decision rules, and MCQs.
- Start each short-answer item directly with its number and scenario. Do not add a separate descriptive mini-heading above the scenario.
- Provide one worked example before independent items when space allows.
- Leave usable response space without large empty areas.

## Preferences inferred from the teacher-edited Version 20

- Prefer a compact structured grid for investment examples, comparing the asset, sources of total return, and ways a loss can occur, rather than listing asset names alone.
- Keep return descriptions theoretically precise: separate income from possible capital gain, never imply that a capital gain is guaranteed, and identify asset-specific loss mechanisms such as default, price falls, costs, vacancy, or fees.
- Include several distinct investment types in the comparison grid so students can transfer the concept beyond one asset class.
- Keep the main definition of speculation short. Two clear sentences are preferable to adding a long qualification inside the definition card.
- Annotations are one source of translation needs, but also audit unannotated terminology. Every difficult technical term must have direct Chinese support or appear in a clearly adjacent bilingual term bank on the same page.
- Avoid recycling the same context across definitions, classification practice, and MCQs when a fresh example can test the same distinction.
- Replace difficult abstractions in Grade 9 answer options, such as `source of payoff` or `holding period`, with accurate but more direct wording.
- Make open-ended questions concrete and answerable by using short decision scenarios, a specific task, and a focused term bank. Retain substantial response space.
- Treat square-bracketed text as editorial guidance to interpret and remove, while still reviewing unannotated content for accuracy, clarity, and layout.
- Prefer a quieter running header with only the course title; do not restore a right-side `Grade 9 | IGCSE bridge` label.

- Consistent table geometry, borders, padding, and vertical alignment.
- No decorative colour dependence.
- Keep headers and footers quiet and consistent.
- Favor readable density over large unused areas or cramped text.
- Keep all text editable in the DOCX; do not rasterize the handout pages.
